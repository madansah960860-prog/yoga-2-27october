Styles of practice

Ten styles of yoga, mapped and compared

“Yoga” on a class schedule can mean anything from a sweaty, fast-moving flow to an hour of lying on bolsters. Here is what each major style involves, where it came from, and who it tends to suit, so you can choose a class with confidence.

01 · The foundation

Hatha Yoga

Pace: slow–moderateClass length: 60–90 minGood for: beginners, all ages

Technically, “hatha” describes all physical yoga. The term comes from medieval texts such as the 15th-century Hatha Yoga Pradipika, which taught postures, breath control and cleansing practices as preparation for meditation. On today’s schedules, a Hatha class usually means a gentler, slower-paced session in which poses are held for several breaths and explained step by step.

What to expect: a short centering, standing and seated poses held individually rather than flowed together, some breathing practice and a final relaxation. It is an ideal place to learn the names and shapes of the poses in our pose library.

02 · Breath in motion

Vinyasa Flow

Pace: moderate–fastClass length: 45–75 minGood for: those who enjoy movement

Vinyasa loosely means “to place in a special way.” In practice it refers to linking each movement to an inhale or an exhale, so that poses flow continuously. Vinyasa grew out of the Ashtanga tradition associated with T. Krishnamacharya and K. Pattabhi Jois, but unlike Ashtanga, sequences change from class to class.

What to expect: sun salutations, flowing standing sequences, some balances and backbends, often with music. Classes range from beginner-friendly to very athletic, so check the level before you go.

03 · Discipline and repetition

Ashtanga Vinyasa Yoga

Pace: fast, set sequenceClass length: 60–120 minGood for: structure-lovers

Ashtanga Vinyasa Yoga was developed and taught by K. Pattabhi Jois in Mysore, India, from the mid-20th century. Students follow the same set series of postures every time, starting with the Primary Series, and add new poses only when ready. The practice combines continuous ujjayi breathing, energetic locks (bandhas) and fixed gaze points (drishti).

What to expect: either a “led” class, where a teacher counts the sequence aloud, or “Mysore style,” where each student practices at their own pace while the teacher offers individual help. It is demanding and rewarding, and it builds a very consistent habit.

04 · Precision and props

Iyengar Yoga

Pace: slow, preciseClass length: 60–120 minGood for: detail-oriented learners

Developed by B.K.S. Iyengar (1918–2014), whose 1966 book Light on Yoga became one of the most influential yoga books ever published, this method emphasizes exact alignment and the creative use of props such as blocks, belts, blankets, chairs and wall ropes. Certified Iyengar teachers complete a long and rigorous training.

What to expect: fewer poses held for longer, detailed instruction and demonstrations, and props that make poses accessible to bodies of every shape. Many teachers of other styles credit Iyengar yoga for their understanding of alignment.

05 · Stillness and time

Yin Yoga

Pace: very slowHolds: 3–5+ minutesGood for: balancing active lifestyles

Yin Yoga is a quiet, floor-based practice in which poses are held passively for several minutes, with the muscles relaxed. It was shaped in the late 20th century by teachers including Paulie Zink, Paul Grilley and Sarah Powers, and it draws on ideas from Taoist practice as well as yoga. The long holds are intended to place gentle, sustained stress on connective tissues around the hips, pelvis and spine.

What to expect: a handful of seated and reclined poses, long silences and a strong sensation of stretch that softens with time. It is physically gentle but mentally challenging, and a natural complement to vigorous styles.

06 · Deep rest

Restorative Yoga

Pace: very slowHolds: 5–20 minutesGood for: rest, recovery, stress

Restorative Yoga uses bolsters, blankets and blocks to support the body completely, so that no muscular effort is required. It grew from B.K.S. Iyengar’s use of props and was popularized by teachers such as Judith Hanson Lasater. Where Yin seeks a gentle stretch, Restorative aims for comfort and complete ease.

What to expect: perhaps five or six poses in an hour, a dim room and plenty of quiet. Many people find it a helpful way to unwind and practice deliberate relaxation.

07 · Breath, sound and meditation

Kundalini Yoga

Pace: variesClass length: 60–90 minGood for: meditation-curious practitioners

Kundalini Yoga, as widely taught in the West since 1969, combines repeated movements, dynamic breathing, chanting (mantra), hand gestures (mudra) and meditation into sets called kriyas. In yogic thought, kundalini refers to a dormant energy at the base of the spine, which these practices aim to awaken.

What to expect: less emphasis on posture alignment and more on breath and repetition, often with music and chanting. Some breathing techniques used, such as rapid “breath of fire,” aren’t suitable for everyone, especially during pregnancy or with heart or blood-pressure conditions.

08 · Strength and sweat

Power Yoga

Pace: fastClass length: 45–75 minGood for: athletes, fitness-focused students

Power Yoga emerged in the United States in the 1990s, when teachers such as Beryl Bender Birch and Bryan Kest adapted the athletic Ashtanga approach for Western gyms and studios without a fixed sequence. It is vigorous, strength-oriented and usually fast-moving.

What to expect: lots of sun salutations, lunges, planks, core work and arm balances. If you are new to exercise, build a foundation in Hatha or beginner Vinyasa first.

09 · Practice in the heat

Hot Yoga

Room: often 90–105°FClass length: 60–90 minGood for: healthy, heat-tolerant adults

Hot yoga refers to any class taught in a heated room. Some studios teach a fixed sequence of 26 postures and two breathing exercises derived from Bikram-style classes; others run heated Vinyasa or Power classes. The heat makes muscles feel more pliable and the practice feel more intense.

What to expect: heavy sweating, a towel over your mat and the need to hydrate well before and after class. Heat raises the risk of dizziness, dehydration and overstretching. Avoid hot yoga during pregnancy and if you have heart conditions, blood-pressure problems or heat sensitivity unless your doctor approves.

10 · Accessible to everyone

Gentle & Chair Yoga

Pace: slowClass length: 30–60 minGood for: older adults, limited mobility, desk workers

Gentle yoga slows everything down, uses simpler variations and offers plenty of rest. Chair yoga adapts poses so they can be done seated or with a chair for support, making yoga available to people who find getting to the floor difficult, including many older adults and anyone recovering their mobility with medical approval.

What to expect: joint-friendly movement, breathing, light strengthening and balance work near a support. Gentle classes are a welcoming entry point at any age.

Choosing a class

How to choose a style

There is no “best” style, only the one that fits your body, goals and temperament right now.

  • Brand new or returning after a break? Start with Hatha, Iyengar, Gentle or a beginner-level Vinyasa class.
  • Want a workout? Vinyasa, Power or Ashtanga will raise your heart rate and build strength.
  • Want to slow down? Yin and Restorative balance a busy or athletic life.
  • Drawn to meditation? Try Kundalini, or explore our breath & meditation guide.
  • Managing an injury or condition? Speak to your doctor first, then look for gentle, prop-based classes and small group sizes.

Not sure which style fits?

In a one-to-one session, a teacher can introduce you to several approaches and help you find the one that suits you.
