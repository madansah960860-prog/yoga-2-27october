Standing poses

Standing poses build the foundation of most practices: strong legs, an organized pelvis and steady breathing. They are the best place for beginners to learn alignment.

Mountain Pose

Tadasana
Level
Beginner
Type
Standing
Hold
5–10 breaths
Focus
Posture, alignment

Mountain Pose looks like simply standing still, yet it holds the blueprint for every standing posture. Learning to stand evenly, with a long spine and a quiet breath, carries straight into Warrior, Triangle and Tree.

How to practice

  1. Stand with feet hip-width apart or big toes touching, the outer edges of the feet parallel.
  2. Spread the toes and press evenly through the big-toe mound, little-toe mound and heel.
  3. Lift the kneecaps gently by engaging the thighs, without locking the knees.
  4. Lengthen the tailbone toward the floor and draw the lower belly slightly in and up.
  5. Broaden the collarbones, relax the shoulders down and let the arms hang with palms facing forward or in.
  6. Balance the head over the pelvis, chin parallel to the floor, and breathe smoothly through the nose.

Why practice it

  • Builds awareness of posture and how weight is distributed through the feet
  • Engages the thighs, core and muscles that support an upright spine
  • Provides a steady, calm starting point between standing poses

Make it easier

Stand with your back against a wall to feel the alignment of heels, pelvis, shoulder blades and the back of the head.

Caution: If you feel light-headed when standing still, keep the feet wider and the knees soft, and sit down if dizziness continues.

Chair Pose

Utkatasana
Level
Beginner
Type
Standing, strength
Hold
5–8 breaths
Focus
Legs, core

Chair Pose is a powerful leg strengthener disguised as a simple squat. It teaches you to bear weight through the heels, keep the spine long under effort and breathe steadily when the thighs start to burn.

How to practice

  1. Begin in Mountain Pose with the feet together or hip-width apart.
  2. Inhale and lift the arms overhead, palms facing each other.
  3. Exhale, bend the knees and sit the hips back as if lowering onto a chair.
  4. Shift your weight into the heels so you could lift your toes.
  5. Keep the knees in line with the toes and draw the lower ribs gently in.
  6. Hold for several breaths, then press through the feet to stand and lower the arms.

Why practice it

  • Strengthens the quadriceps, glutes, calves and ankles
  • Builds endurance and the habit of steady breathing under effort
  • Engages the core muscles and the muscles along the spine

Make it easier

Keep the hands at the heart or on the thighs, or practice with your back sliding down a wall.

Caution: Bend the knees less if you have knee discomfort, and lower the arms if you have shoulder pain or high blood pressure.

Standing Forward Fold

Uttanasana
Level
Beginner
Type
Standing, forward bend
Hold
5–10 breaths
Focus
Hamstrings, back

Standing Forward Fold releases the back of the legs and lets the spine hang with gravity. It appears in sun salutations, and it is one of the most effective poses for noticing tension in the hamstrings.

How to practice

  1. Stand in Mountain Pose with the feet hip-width apart.
  2. Place the hands on the hips, inhale and lengthen the spine.
  3. Exhale and hinge forward from the hip joints, not the waist.
  4. Bend the knees as much as needed so the belly can rest toward the thighs.
  5. Let the head and neck relax; place the hands on the floor, blocks or shins.
  6. To come up, bend the knees, engage the belly and rise slowly with a long back.

Why practice it

  • Stretches the hamstrings, calves and muscles along the back
  • Gives the neck and shoulders a chance to release
  • Many people find the forward, downward position quieting

Make it easier

Bend the knees generously and rest the forearms on the thighs, or place the hands on blocks.

Caution: Avoid deep folding with a recent back injury or disc problem, and rise slowly if you have low blood pressure. Keep the head above the heart if you have glaucoma.

Warrior I

Virabhadrasana I
Level
Beginner
Type
Standing
Hold
5–8 breaths each side
Focus
Legs, hip flexors

Warrior I combines a strong lunge with a lift through the chest and arms. Named after the mythic warrior Virabhadra, it builds leg strength while stretching the hip flexors of the back leg.

How to practice

  1. From Mountain Pose, step one foot about three to four feet back.
  2. Turn the back foot out about 45 degrees and press its outer edge down.
  3. Bend the front knee toward a right angle, keeping it over the ankle.
  4. Square the hips toward the front as far as is comfortable.
  5. Inhale and raise the arms overhead, shoulders relaxed away from the ears.
  6. Hold, then straighten the front leg and step forward. Repeat on the other side.

Why practice it

  • Strengthens the legs, glutes and the muscles around the knees
  • Stretches the hip flexors and calf of the back leg
  • Opens the chest and encourages full, easy breathing

Make it easier

Take a wider stance side to side (like train tracks) for balance, or lift the back heel into a high lunge.

Caution: Keep the front knee over the ankle, not past the toes. Use a shorter stance if you have knee or hip pain, and keep the hands on the hips if raising the arms strains the shoulders.

Warrior II

Virabhadrasana II
Level
Beginner
Type
Standing
Hold
5–8 breaths each side
Focus
Legs, hips, stamina

Warrior II is a wide, open stance that asks for strength and stillness at the same time. Its gaze over the front fingertips makes it a classic pose for training concentration.

How to practice

  1. Stand with the feet wide apart, about the length of one leg.
  2. Turn the front foot to face forward and the back foot in slightly.
  3. Align the front heel with the arch of the back foot.
  4. Extend the arms out to the sides at shoulder height, palms down.
  5. Exhale and bend the front knee over the ankle, tracking toward the little toes.
  6. Keep the torso upright over the pelvis and gaze past the front middle finger.

Why practice it

  • Strengthens the thighs, glutes and ankles
  • Opens the hips and inner thighs
  • Builds stamina and focus as you hold a steady gaze

Make it easier

Shorten the stance, bend the front knee less, or rest the hands on the hips.

Caution: Let the front knee point toward the second and third toes rather than collapse inward. Reduce the depth of the bend if you feel knee strain.

Triangle Pose

Utthita Trikonasana
Level
Beginner
Type
Standing, lateral stretch
Hold
5–8 breaths each side
Focus
Hamstrings, side body

Triangle Pose creates long lines through the legs and the side of the body. Its secret is length, not depth: the goal is a long spine, not touching the floor.

How to practice

  1. Begin with the feet wide apart, front foot pointing forward, back foot turned in slightly.
  2. Extend the arms to shoulder height.
  3. Reach the front arm forward, shifting the hips back, then tilt the torso over the front leg.
  4. Rest the lower hand on the shin, a block or the floor outside the front foot.
  5. Stack the top shoulder over the bottom and reach the top arm toward the ceiling.
  6. Keep both legs straight but not locked. Gaze up, forward or down, whichever is comfortable for your neck.

Why practice it

  • Stretches the hamstrings, groins and side waist
  • Strengthens the legs and the muscles that stabilize the trunk
  • Opens the chest and shoulders

Make it easier

Place the bottom hand high on the shin or on a block, and practice with your back body lightly against a wall.

Caution: Do not lock the front knee. If you have neck problems, look forward or down rather than up at the top hand.

Extended Side Angle

Utthita Parsvakonasana
Level
Beginner–Intermediate
Type
Standing, lateral stretch
Hold
5–8 breaths each side
Focus
Legs, side body

Extended Side Angle grows out of Warrior II. With the front knee bent and the top arm reaching overhead, it forms one long diagonal line from the back heel to the fingertips.

How to practice

  1. Come into Warrior II with the front knee bent over the ankle.
  2. Lower the front forearm onto the thigh, or the hand to a block or the floor.
  3. Press the outer edge of the back foot down firmly.
  4. Extend the top arm over the ear, palm facing down.
  5. Rotate the chest gently toward the ceiling, keeping the front knee steady.
  6. Breathe into the long side of the body, then press up and repeat on the other side.

Why practice it

  • Strengthens the legs, knees and ankles
  • Stretches the side body, groins and shoulders
  • Builds coordination between lower-body strength and upper-body openness

Make it easier

Use the forearm-on-thigh variation, and keep the top hand on the hip.

Caution: Rest the forearm on the thigh rather than dropping into the lower shoulder. Look down if turning the head is uncomfortable for your neck.

Balancing poses

Balances train focus, ankle and hip stability, and the small stabilizing muscles that keep us steady in daily life. Practice near a wall and expect to wobble; wobbling is part of the training.

Tree Pose

Vrksasana
Level
Beginner
Type
Standing balance
Hold
5–10 breaths each side
Focus
Balance, focus

Tree Pose is the classic first balance. Rooting down through one foot while the rest of the body grows tall is a simple, honest test of focus, and it gets steadier with practice. Shown here with the arms extended wide.

How to practice

  1. Stand in Mountain Pose and shift your weight into the left foot.
  2. Bend the right knee and place the sole of the right foot on the left ankle, calf or inner thigh, but never on the knee.
  3. Press the foot and standing leg into each other to stay centered.
  4. Bring the hands to the heart, or extend them overhead or out to the sides.
  5. Fix your gaze on a still point in front of you.
  6. Hold, release with control and repeat on the other side.

Why practice it

  • Improves balance and ankle stability
  • Strengthens the standing leg and hip stabilizers
  • Trains steady attention and concentration

Make it easier

Keep the toes of the lifted foot on the floor like a kickstand, with the heel on the ankle, or touch a wall for support.

Caution: Avoid pressing the foot into the side of the knee joint. Practice beside a wall or chair if you have balance issues or low blood pressure.

Eagle Pose

Garudasana
Level
Intermediate
Type
Standing balance
Hold
5–8 breaths each side
Focus
Balance, shoulders

Eagle Pose wraps the arms and legs around each other while you balance on a bent standing leg. It is compact and demanding, and it stretches the upper back in a way few other poses do.

How to practice

  1. From Mountain Pose, bend both knees slightly.
  2. Lift the right leg and cross the right thigh over the left.
  3. If possible, hook the right foot behind the left calf; otherwise rest the toes on the floor.
  4. Extend the arms forward, cross the left arm over the right and bend the elbows.
  5. Bring the palms together if you can, or hold opposite shoulders.
  6. Sit a little lower, lift the elbows to shoulder height and hold, then unwind and switch sides.

Why practice it

  • Strengthens the ankles, calves and thighs
  • Stretches the upper back, shoulders and outer hips
  • Demands and develops concentration

Make it easier

Hug the shoulders instead of wrapping the forearms, and rest the top foot's toes on the floor or on a block.

Caution: Skip the leg wrap if you have knee pain, and avoid forcing the arm wrap with a shoulder injury.

Warrior III

Virabhadrasana III
Level
Intermediate
Type
Standing balance
Hold
3–6 breaths each side
Focus
Balance, posterior chain

Warrior III turns the body into a T, balancing on one leg with the torso and lifted leg parallel to the floor. It is a full-body strength pose that rewards patience over force.

How to practice

  1. From a high lunge, shift your weight onto the front foot.
  2. Hinge forward at the hips as you lift the back leg behind you.
  3. Keep the hips level, with the toes of the lifted foot pointing down.
  4. Extend the arms forward, out to the sides, or keep the hands at the heart.
  5. Lengthen from the heel of the lifted leg to the crown of the head.
  6. Hold, then lower the leg with control and repeat on the other side.

Why practice it

  • Strengthens the standing leg, glutes, hamstrings and back muscles
  • Improves balance and coordination
  • Engages the core to stabilize the trunk

Make it easier

Place the hands on blocks or the back of a chair, or keep the lifted foot lightly on a wall behind you.

Caution: Keep a micro-bend in the standing knee. Avoid it if you have an ankle injury or problems with balance or blood pressure that make the head-forward position uncomfortable.

Half Moon Pose

Ardha Chandrasana
Level
Intermediate
Type
Standing balance
Hold
3–6 breaths each side
Focus
Balance, hips

Half Moon balances on one hand and one foot while the body opens sideways like a sliver of the moon. A block under the hand makes it accessible far sooner than most people expect.

How to practice

  1. Begin in Triangle Pose on the right side, with a block nearby.
  2. Bend the front knee and place the right hand on the floor or block about a foot ahead of the front foot.
  3. Shift your weight forward and lift the left leg until it is parallel to the floor.
  4. Straighten the standing leg and stack the left hip over the right.
  5. Reach the left arm up, opening the chest toward the side.
  6. Hold, then bend the standing knee and lower the leg back to Triangle. Repeat on the other side.

Why practice it

  • Strengthens the ankles, legs and glutes
  • Stretches the hamstrings, groins and chest
  • Builds balance, coordination and spatial awareness

Make it easier

Use a block under the hand, keep the top hand on the hip, or practice with your back against a wall.

Caution: Do not lock the standing knee. Look down at the floor if turning the head is uncomfortable for your neck.

Dancer Pose

Natarajasana
Level
Intermediate–Advanced
Type
Standing balance, backbend
Hold
3–6 breaths each side
Focus
Balance, quads, chest

Dancer Pose, named after Nataraja, the dancing form of Shiva, combines a standing balance with a graceful backbend. It is as much about finding a steady kick into the hand as it is about flexibility.

How to practice

  1. Stand in Mountain Pose and shift your weight onto the left foot.
  2. Bend the right knee and hold the inside of the right foot or ankle with the right hand.
  3. Reach the left arm forward and up.
  4. Press the foot back into the hand, lifting the leg behind you.
  5. Tip the torso forward only as far as you can keep the chest lifted.
  6. Hold, then release slowly and switch sides.

Why practice it

  • Strengthens the standing leg and ankle
  • Stretches the quadriceps, hip flexors, chest and shoulders
  • Develops balance and graceful control

Make it easier

Loop a strap around the back foot, and hold a wall or chair with the free hand.

Caution: Kick into the hand gradually rather than pulling the foot. Avoid with a low-back injury or if backbends are not advised for you.

Crow Pose

Bakasana
Level
Intermediate
Type
Arm balance
Hold
2–5 breaths
Focus
Arms, core

Crow Pose is many students' first arm balance. It depends less on raw strength than on learning to lean forward, rounding the back and trusting the hands. A pillow in front of you helps with nerves.

How to practice

  1. Squat with the feet close together and the knees wide.
  2. Place the hands shoulder-width apart on the mat in front of you, fingers spread.
  3. Bend the elbows slightly back and place the knees high on the backs of the upper arms.
  4. Round the upper back, draw the belly in and gaze slightly forward.
  5. Shift your weight forward into the hands and lift one foot, then the other.
  6. Hold briefly, then lower the feet with control.

Why practice it

  • Strengthens the wrists, forearms, shoulders and core
  • Builds balance and body awareness
  • Develops confidence for other arm balances

Make it easier

Stand the feet on a block to start higher, and practice lifting one foot at a time.

Caution: Avoid with wrist injuries or carpal tunnel symptoms, and during pregnancy. Place a cushion in front of your face while learning.

Core & arm-support poses

These poses develop strength through the shoulders, arms and trunk. Build them gradually and keep the shoulders broad, so the work is shared by the whole body instead of dumped into the wrists.

Downward-Facing Dog

Adho Mukha Svanasana
Level
Beginner
Type
Inversion (mild), stretch
Hold
5–10 breaths
Focus
Hamstrings, shoulders

Downward-Facing Dog is the most recognizable pose in modern yoga and a frequent resting point in flowing classes. It strengthens the arms and shoulders while stretching the entire back of the body.

How to practice

  1. Start on hands and knees with the wrists under the shoulders and knees under the hips.
  2. Spread the fingers and press firmly through the knuckles and fingertips.
  3. Tuck the toes, lift the knees and send the hips up and back.
  4. Keep the knees bent at first to lengthen the spine.
  5. Straighten the legs only as far as the back stays long, heels reaching toward the floor.
  6. Relax the neck, with the head between the upper arms, and breathe steadily.

Why practice it

  • Stretches the hamstrings, calves and the muscles along the spine
  • Strengthens the arms, shoulders and upper back
  • Gives a mild, accessible inversion as the head drops below the heart

Make it easier

Keep the knees deeply bent, or place the hands on blocks or a chair seat to reduce weight in the wrists.

Caution: Avoid or modify if you have wrist or shoulder injuries, high blood pressure, or eye conditions such as glaucoma. Come down to Child's Pose if you feel pressure in the head.

Plank Pose

Phalakasana
Level
Beginner
Type
Arm support, core
Hold
20–60 seconds
Focus
Core, shoulders

Plank is the bridge between many poses in a sun salutation. It teaches you to hold a long, neutral spine, a skill that protects the lower back in nearly everything else you do.

How to practice

  1. From hands and knees, place the wrists under the shoulders.
  2. Step the feet back one at a time until the body forms a straight line.
  3. Press the floor away so the shoulder blades spread slightly.
  4. Draw the lower belly in and lengthen the tailbone toward the heels.
  5. Keep the neck long, gazing slightly ahead of the hands.
  6. Hold with steady breathing, then lower the knees to rest.

Why practice it

  • Strengthens the abdominals, shoulders, arms and wrists
  • Teaches a neutral, supported spine
  • Builds endurance for flowing practice

Make it easier

Lower the knees to the floor, or practice a forearm plank to take weight off the wrists.

Caution: Avoid or shorten the hold with wrist or shoulder pain. Stop if the lower back starts to sag.

Side Plank

Vasisthasana
Level
Intermediate
Type
Arm balance, core
Hold
3–6 breaths each side
Focus
Obliques, shoulders

Side Plank turns the body sideways onto one arm. It builds the side-body strength that front-facing exercises miss and challenges shoulder stability. Shown here in a lifted variation.

How to practice

  1. Begin in Plank Pose.
  2. Shift your weight onto the right hand and roll onto the outer edge of the right foot.
  3. Stack the left foot on top of the right, or stagger the feet for balance.
  4. Lift the hips so the body forms one long line.
  5. Reach the left arm toward the ceiling.
  6. Hold, return to Plank and repeat on the other side.

Why practice it

  • Strengthens the obliques, shoulders and wrists
  • Builds stability around the shoulder joint
  • Improves balance and coordination

Make it easier

Lower the bottom knee to the floor, or practice on the forearm.

Caution: Avoid with wrist, elbow or shoulder injuries. Keep the supporting hand slightly ahead of the shoulder to reduce strain.

Boat Pose

Navasana
Level
Intermediate
Type
Seated balance, core
Hold
3–6 breaths
Focus
Core, hip flexors

Boat Pose balances on the sit bones with the legs and chest lifted. It strengthens the deep abdominal and hip flexor muscles, and most of the work is in keeping the chest lifted rather than collapsing.

How to practice

  1. Sit with the knees bent and the feet on the floor.
  2. Hold behind the thighs, lift the chest and lean back slightly.
  3. Lift the feet so the shins are parallel to the floor.
  4. Release the hands and extend the arms forward at shoulder height.
  5. If the back stays long, straighten the legs into a V shape.
  6. Hold, breathe, then lower the feet and rest.

Why practice it

  • Strengthens the abdominal muscles, hip flexors and spine
  • Builds balance and steadiness
  • Develops posture awareness when sitting

Make it easier

Keep the knees bent and hold the backs of the thighs, or keep the toes lightly on the floor.

Caution: Avoid with a recent back injury, during pregnancy, or if you have had recent abdominal surgery.

Backbends

Backbends open the front of the body and strengthen the back. Warm up first, lengthen before you bend, and treat the spine as one long curve rather than folding at the lower back.

Cat–Cow

Marjaryasana–Bitilasana
Level
Beginner
Type
Spinal movement
Hold
5–10 rounds
Focus
Spine, breath

Cat–Cow pairs two movements with the breath: arching and rounding the spine. It is a gentle warm-up used in almost every style of yoga.

How to practice

  1. Start on hands and knees, wrists under shoulders and knees under hips.
  2. Inhale (Cow): let the belly lower, lift the chest and tailbone and look slightly forward.
  3. Exhale (Cat): press the floor away, round the spine toward the ceiling and drop the head.
  4. Move slowly, letting the breath set the pace.
  5. Repeat for five to ten rounds, then return to a neutral spine.

Why practice it

  • Gently mobilizes the whole spine
  • Coordinates movement and breathing
  • Warms up the back, shoulders and neck before practice

Make it easier

Practice seated in a chair, with the hands on the knees, arching and rounding the back.

Caution: Keep the movement small with a neck injury, holding the head in line with the spine. Pad the knees if kneeling is uncomfortable.

Cobra Pose

Bhujangasana
Level
Beginner
Type
Backbend
Hold
3–6 breaths
Focus
Back strength, chest

Cobra is a gentle, accessible backbend that strengthens the muscles along the spine. The lift should come mainly from the back, not from pushing hard with the arms.

How to practice

  1. Lie face down with the legs extended and the tops of the feet on the mat.
  2. Place the hands under the shoulders, elbows close to the ribs.
  3. Press the pubic bone and the tops of the feet into the floor.
  4. Inhale and lift the head and chest, using the back muscles more than the arms.
  5. Keep the elbows bent, shoulders away from the ears and neck long.
  6. Exhale and lower slowly.

Why practice it

  • Strengthens the muscles along the spine
  • Stretches the chest, shoulders and abdomen
  • Counteracts the forward-rounded posture of long hours at a desk

Make it easier

Practice Sphinx Pose instead, resting on the forearms with the elbows under the shoulders.

Caution: Avoid during pregnancy and with recent abdominal surgery. Lift only to a low height if you have lower-back discomfort.

Upward-Facing Dog

Urdhva Mukha Svanasana
Level
Intermediate
Type
Backbend
Hold
2–5 breaths
Focus
Chest, arms

Upward-Facing Dog is a stronger cousin of Cobra in which the thighs and hips lift off the floor. It is a staple of vinyasa sun salutations.

How to practice

  1. Lie face down with the hands beside the lower ribs.
  2. Press the tops of the feet down and engage the legs.
  3. Inhale and straighten the arms, lifting the chest, hips and thighs off the floor.
  4. Keep the shoulders over the wrists and draw them back and down.
  5. Lift through the breastbone, keeping the neck long.
  6. Exhale and lower, or press back to Downward-Facing Dog.

Why practice it

  • Strengthens the arms, wrists and spinal muscles
  • Stretches the chest, abdomen and hip flexors
  • Builds strength for flowing sequences

Make it easier

Keep the thighs on the floor and practice Cobra instead.

Caution: Avoid with wrist or lower-back injuries and during pregnancy. Do not let the shoulders creep toward the ears.

Bow Pose

Dhanurasana
Level
Intermediate
Type
Backbend
Hold
3–5 breaths
Focus
Chest, quads, back

Bow Pose draws the body into the shape of an archer's bow, with the hands holding the ankles. It opens the entire front body and strengthens the back.

How to practice

  1. Lie face down with the arms beside the body.
  2. Bend the knees and reach back to hold the outsides of the ankles.
  3. Keep the knees about hip-width apart.
  4. Inhale and press the ankles into the hands, lifting the thighs and chest.
  5. Gaze forward and breathe steadily into the back of the ribs.
  6. Exhale, release slowly and rest with the head turned to one side.

Why practice it

  • Stretches the chest, abdomen, hip flexors and quadriceps
  • Strengthens the back muscles
  • Opens the shoulders

Make it easier

Loop a strap around the ankles, or practice Half Bow, lifting one leg at a time.

Caution: Avoid during pregnancy, with recent abdominal surgery, or with lower-back or neck injuries.

Camel Pose

Ustrasana
Level
Intermediate
Type
Kneeling backbend
Hold
3–5 breaths
Focus
Chest, hip flexors

Camel Pose is a kneeling backbend that opens the chest and the front of the hips. It can feel intense physically and emotionally, so enter it slowly and come out with even more care.

How to practice

  1. Kneel with the knees hip-width apart and the hips over the knees.
  2. Place the hands on the lower back, fingers pointing down.
  3. Inhale and lift the chest, drawing the elbows toward each other.
  4. Keep the hips moving forward as the upper back bends.
  5. If comfortable, reach the hands back to the heels, with the toes tucked if needed.
  6. To come out, return the hands to the hips and lift up with the chest leading. Rest in Child's Pose.

Why practice it

  • Stretches the chest, abdomen, hip flexors and the fronts of the thighs
  • Strengthens the back muscles
  • Encourages fuller breathing into the front of the chest

Make it easier

Keep the hands on the lower back, or rest them on blocks placed beside the ankles.

Caution: Avoid with neck or lower-back injuries, high or low blood pressure, or migraine. Keep the chin slightly tucked if dropping the head back is uncomfortable.

Bridge Pose

Setu Bandha Sarvangasana
Level
Beginner
Type
Backbend
Hold
5–8 breaths
Focus
Glutes, chest

Bridge Pose lifts the hips from the floor to create a gentle, supported backbend. It is an excellent pose for strengthening the glutes and the back of the legs.

How to practice

  1. Lie on your back with the knees bent and the feet flat, hip-width apart.
  2. Bring the heels close enough that the fingertips can graze them.
  3. Press down through the feet and arms and lift the hips.
  4. Keep the knees pointing forward, in line with the hips.
  5. Interlace the hands beneath you, or keep the arms long by your sides.
  6. Hold, then roll down slowly one vertebra at a time.

Why practice it

  • Strengthens the glutes, hamstrings and back
  • Stretches the chest, hip flexors and the front of the thighs
  • A gentle introduction to backbending

Make it easier

Place a yoga block under the sacrum for a supported, restful version.

Caution: Don't turn the head while in the pose. Avoid it with neck injuries, and support the pelvis with a block if you feel strain.

Wheel Pose

Urdhva Dhanurasana
Level
Advanced
Type
Backbend
Hold
2–5 breaths
Focus
Shoulders, spine

Wheel Pose is a full backbend from the floor, pressing up onto hands and feet. It requires open shoulders, strong legs and a good warm-up, and it is not a pose to force.

How to practice

  1. Lie on your back with the knees bent and the heels close to the hips.
  2. Place the hands beside the ears, fingers pointing toward the shoulders.
  3. Press into the feet and lift the hips.
  4. Press into the hands and lift onto the crown of the head briefly, keeping the elbows parallel.
  5. Straighten the arms to lift the head off the floor.
  6. To come down, tuck the chin and lower slowly onto the back.

Why practice it

  • Strengthens the arms, shoulders, glutes and legs
  • Stretches the chest, abdomen and hip flexors
  • Builds strength and confidence for deeper backbends

Make it easier

Practice Bridge Pose, or place two blocks against a wall under the hands for extra height.

Caution: Avoid with back, wrist or shoulder injuries, high blood pressure, headache, or during pregnancy. Warm up thoroughly first.

Seated poses, hip openers & twists

Seated postures support breathing practice and meditation, and gently mobilize the hips and spine. Sit on a folded blanket or block whenever your knees are higher than your hips.

Easy Seat

Sukhasana
Level
Beginner
Type
Seated
Hold
1–10 minutes
Focus
Posture, meditation

Easy Seat is the simple cross-legged position used for breathing practice and meditation. Its name means comfortable seat, and comfort is the goal, so props are encouraged.

How to practice

  1. Sit on the edge of a folded blanket or cushion.
  2. Cross the shins and slide each foot beneath the opposite knee.
  3. Let the knees relax toward the floor; support them with blocks if they are high.
  4. Root down through the sit bones and lengthen the spine upward.
  5. Rest the hands on the thighs or in the lap, and relax the shoulders.
  6. Switch the cross of the legs halfway through long sits.

Why practice it

  • Encourages an upright, relaxed posture for breathing and meditation
  • Gently opens the hips and knees
  • Offers a calm, grounded position for reflection

Make it easier

Sit higher on a cushion or block, or sit on a chair with both feet flat on the floor.

Caution: Raise the seat if you feel strain in the knees or lower back. Sit in a chair if crossing the legs is uncomfortable.

Bound Angle Pose

Baddha Konasana
Level
Beginner
Type
Seated, hip opener
Hold
1–3 minutes
Focus
Inner thighs, hips

Bound Angle, sometimes called Cobbler's Pose, brings the soles of the feet together and lets the knees fall open. It is a gentle hip opener suitable for most practitioners.

How to practice

  1. Sit tall, on a folded blanket if needed.
  2. Bend the knees and bring the soles of the feet together.
  3. Let the knees fall open to the sides.
  4. Hold the feet or ankles and lengthen the spine.
  5. Stay upright, or hinge forward from the hips with a long back.
  6. Breathe steadily and let the hips soften with each exhale.

Why practice it

  • Stretches the inner thighs, groins and knees
  • Gently mobilizes the hips
  • Supports an upright, comfortable seated posture

Make it easier

Place blocks or cushions under each knee, or slide the feet further from the body.

Caution: Do not press the knees down with your hands. Support the outer thighs with blocks if you have knee or groin discomfort.

Seated Forward Bend

Paschimottanasana
Level
Beginner–Intermediate
Type
Seated forward bend
Hold
1–3 minutes
Focus
Hamstrings, back

Seated Forward Bend stretches the entire back of the body. It works best approached slowly, with patience; pulling yourself down with the arms only creates resistance.

How to practice

  1. Sit with the legs extended forward, on a folded blanket if the lower back rounds.
  2. Flex the feet and press the backs of the legs gently down.
  3. Inhale and lengthen the spine, raising the arms if you like.
  4. Exhale and hinge forward from the hips, keeping the chest open.
  5. Hold the shins, ankles or feet, or loop a strap around the feet.
  6. Relax the head and shoulders and breathe into the back body.

Why practice it

  • Stretches the hamstrings, calves and spine
  • Encourages a slower, quieter breath
  • Many people find it a calming way to wind down a practice

Make it easier

Sit on a blanket, bend the knees and loop a strap around the feet.

Caution: Avoid deep forward bending with a disc injury or acute lower-back pain. Bend the knees if you feel pulling behind them.

Pigeon Pose

Eka Pada Rajakapotasana (variations)
Level
Intermediate
Type
Hip opener
Hold
1–2 minutes each side
Focus
Outer hips, hip flexors

Pigeon Pose stretches the outer hip of the front leg and the hip flexor of the back leg. Its simplest form rests forward over the front shin; the photo shows a more advanced, upright variation.

How to practice

  1. From Downward-Facing Dog, bring the right knee toward the right wrist.
  2. Lay the right shin on the mat; the foot can rest near the left hip, or move forward if comfortable.
  3. Extend the left leg straight back, with the top of the foot on the mat.
  4. Square the hips, using a blanket under the right hip if it lifts.
  5. Stay upright on the hands, or fold forward onto the forearms.
  6. Breathe for one to two minutes, then switch sides.

Why practice it

  • Stretches the outer hips, glutes and hip flexors
  • Increases hip mobility over time
  • Folded forward, it offers a quiet, introspective rest

Make it easier

Practice Reclined Pigeon on your back, or support the front hip with a folded blanket.

Caution: Avoid with knee injuries or sacroiliac pain. If the front knee hurts, choose Figure Four (Reclined Pigeon) on your back instead.

Half Lord of the Fishes

Ardha Matsyendrasana
Level
Beginner–Intermediate
Type
Seated twist
Hold
5–8 breaths each side
Focus
Spine, side body

This seated twist is named after the sage Matsyendra. Twists are traditionally taught as a way to rotate and refresh the spine, with length always coming before rotation.

How to practice

  1. Sit with both legs extended.
  2. Bend the right knee and place the right foot outside the left thigh.
  3. Keep the left leg straight, or bend it so the left foot rests by the right hip.
  4. Inhale and lift the left arm, lengthening the spine.
  5. Exhale and twist toward the right, hugging the right knee or hooking the left elbow outside it.
  6. Lengthen on each inhale and twist a little more on each exhale, then repeat on the other side.

Why practice it

  • Gently rotates and mobilizes the spine
  • Stretches the shoulders, hips and outer thighs
  • Encourages upright posture

Make it easier

Keep the bottom leg straight, sit on a blanket, or practice a gentle twist in a chair.

Caution: Twist from the ribcage, not the neck. Avoid deep twists during pregnancy and with spinal disc injuries.

Inversions

Inversions place the hips above the heart. They demand preparation, shoulder strength and good judgment, and are best learned with a qualified teacher. Never practice them with neck pain.

Supported Shoulderstand

Salamba Sarvangasana
Level
Intermediate–Advanced
Type
Inversion
Hold
10 breaths–3 minutes
Focus
Shoulders, legs

Supported Shoulderstand rests on the shoulders and upper arms with the legs pointing up. It is traditionally practiced with folded blankets under the shoulders to protect the neck, and should be learned with a teacher.

How to practice

  1. Lie on your back with the shoulders on two folded blankets and the head on the floor.
  2. Bend the knees and bring the feet toward the hips.
  3. Lift the hips and place the hands on the lower back for support.
  4. Walk the hands toward the shoulder blades and lift the legs toward the ceiling.
  5. Keep the neck still; do not turn the head.
  6. To come down, bend the knees and roll down slowly with control.

Why practice it

  • Strengthens the shoulders, upper back and legs
  • Reverses the usual effect of gravity on the legs
  • Requires and develops focus and body awareness

Make it easier

Practice Legs Up the Wall instead: lie on your back with the legs resting vertically against a wall.

Caution: Do not practice with neck injuries, high blood pressure, glaucoma or other eye conditions, heart conditions, or during pregnancy. Never turn the head while in the pose.

Supported Headstand

Salamba Sirsasana
Level
Advanced
Type
Inversion
Hold
10 breaths–3 minutes
Focus
Shoulders, core

Headstand is sometimes called the king of poses, but it should be approached with respect. Most of the weight is carried through the forearms and shoulders, not the head, and preparation matters more than the full pose.

How to practice

  1. Kneel and place the forearms on the mat, interlacing the fingers with the elbows shoulder-width apart.
  2. Place the crown of the head on the floor with the back of the head cupped in the hands.
  3. Lift the knees and walk the feet toward the face until the hips are over the shoulders.
  4. Press firmly through the forearms and lift the shoulders away from the floor.
  5. Bend the knees and lift the feet, then extend the legs upward when stable.
  6. Come down slowly with bent knees and rest in Child's Pose.

Why practice it

  • Strengthens the shoulders, arms and core
  • Builds balance, focus and body awareness
  • Offers a strong inversion for experienced practitioners

Make it easier

Practice Dolphin Pose (forearm Downward Dog) to build strength, or work near a wall with an experienced teacher.

Caution: Do not practice with neck injuries, high blood pressure, heart conditions, glaucoma or detached retina, or during pregnancy. Learn it with a qualified teacher; it is not recommended to teach yourself.

Resting poses

Resting poses are not an afterthought. They let the nervous system settle, give you a place to return between effortful poses, and close every practice.

Child's Pose

Balasana
Level
Beginner
Type
Resting pose
Hold
30 seconds–3 minutes
Focus
Back, hips, rest

Child's Pose is the home base of a yoga class. Whenever you need a break, you can return here. It gently stretches the back and hips while letting the breath settle.

How to practice

  1. Kneel with the big toes touching and the knees together or wide.
  2. Sit the hips back toward the heels.
  3. Fold forward and rest the forehead on the mat, a block or stacked fists.
  4. Stretch the arms forward, or rest them alongside the body.
  5. Breathe into the back of the ribs.
  6. Stay as long as you like, then walk the hands back to sit up slowly.

Why practice it

  • Gently stretches the lower back, hips and thighs
  • Offers a restful pause between more effortful poses
  • Encourages slow breathing into the back body

Make it easier

Rest the torso on a bolster or stack of pillows, with the knees wide.

Caution: Widen the knees during pregnancy. Place a cushion behind the knees or under the hips if you have knee discomfort.

Corpse Pose

Savasana
Level
Beginner
Type
Resting pose
Hold
5–15 minutes
Focus
Relaxation, integration

Savasana closes almost every yoga practice. Lying still, without effort, sounds simple, but many practitioners consider it the most important and most challenging pose of all.

How to practice

  1. Lie on your back with the legs extended and the feet falling open.
  2. Rest the arms a little away from the body, palms facing up.
  3. Tuck the shoulder blades slightly under and lengthen the back of the neck.
  4. Close the eyes and let the breath become natural.
  5. Scan the body from feet to head, releasing any effort you notice.
  6. To finish, deepen the breath, roll to one side and sit up slowly.

Why practice it

  • Allows the body and breath to settle after practice
  • Practices the skill of deliberate relaxation
  • Creates a quiet transition back to the rest of your day

Make it easier

Place a rolled blanket under the knees and a folded blanket under the head; cover yourself to stay warm.

Caution: Bend the knees or place a bolster under them if your lower back is uncomfortable. During later pregnancy, rest on your side instead.
